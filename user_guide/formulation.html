Mathematical formulation#

This page documents the linear economic dispatch model utilized by gridops.

Overview#

gridops solves a linear program (LP) that decides, hour by hour, how much power each generator produces, how much each storage unit charges or discharges and how power flows over the transmission network, so that demand is met at the lowest total operating cost.

The main modeling choices are:

  • Hourly time step: Temporal resolution is hourly. Because each interval lasts one hour, a power level in MW equals an energy amount in MWh, and costs in $/MWh multiply power directly.

  • Rolling horizon: The simulation period is split into horizons of \(H\) hours (horizon_hours). The same LP is solved for every horizon. Only the time-varying parameters change between horizons, together with the generator output and storage state of charge carried over from the end of the previous horizon (see Running simulations).

  • Economic dispatch: Generators have no on/off (commitment) decisions, minimum stable output, start-up or no-load costs, minimum up and down times, so the model stays linear and continuous.

  • DC power flow: The network is represented by the linearized DC power flow approximation. The flow on a transmissionline is proportional to the difference in voltage angle between its two ends.

  • Load shedding as a last resort: Each bus can shed load at a high penalty cost, so the problem always has a feasible solution. The load shedding amount is reported as unserved energy.

Nomenclature#

Calligraphic letters denote sets, upper-case letters denote parameters and lower-case letters denote decision variables. Subscripts give the index: \(g\) for generators, \(b\) for buses, \(l\) for lines, \(s\) for storage units, \(k\) for outage groups and \(t\) for hours.

Objective#

The objective is to minimize the total system operating cost over the horizon:

\[\min \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \Bigg[ \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}^{TH}} \left( HR_g \, FP_g + VOM_g \right) p_{g,t} + \gamma^{LS} \sum_{b \in \mathcal{B}} u_{b,t} + \gamma^{R} \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}^{HY} \cup \mathcal{G}^{VRE}} p_{g,t} + \gamma^{F} \sum_{l \in \mathcal{L}} \hat{f}_{l,t} + \gamma^{ST} \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \left( c_{s,t} + d_{s,t} \right) \Bigg]\]

The five terms are:

  1. Thermal generation cost: The marginal cost of a thermal unit is the fuel it burns per MWh (heat rate (\(HR_g\))) times the fuel price (\(FP_g\)), plus the variable O&M cost (\(VOM_g\)). This determines the merit order so that cheaper units are dispatched first.

  2. Unserved energy penalty: Cost penalty for load shedding (\(\gamma^{LS}\)) is well above the marginal cost of generators, so the model sheds load only when no combination of generation, storage and transmission can meet demand.

  3. Hydro and renewable cost: Hydro, solar, onshore wind, and offshore wind have no fuel cost. A small cost (\(\gamma^{R}\)) breaks ties between these zero-cost resources.

  4. Absolute-flow penalty: A small cost (\(\gamma^{F}\)) on the absolute value of every line flow discourages needless circulating flows that don’t change the total cost. This also discourages moving unserved energy through the network unnecessarily.

  5. Storage cycling cost: A small cost (\(\gamma^{ST}\)) on charging and discharging stops a unit from charging and discharging in the same hour, which would otherwise waste energy through the efficiency losses at no cost.

Constraints#

Bus power balance#

At every bus and in every hour, the power injected must equal the power withdrawn (Kirchhoff’s current law):

\[\sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_b} p_{g,t} + u_{b,t} + MR_{b,t} - \sum_{l \in \mathcal{L}} A_{l,b} \, f_{l,t} = D_{b,t} + \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_b} \left( c_{s,t} - d_{s,t} \right) \qquad \forall b \in \mathcal{B},\ t \in \mathcal{T}\]

The left-hand side is the supply at the bus: generation from the bus’s generators, unserved energy, must-run output, minus the net power leaving the bus over transmission lines. Because \(A_{l,b} = +1\) at a line’s from bus and \(-1\) at its to bus, \(\sum_{l} A_{l,b} f_{l,t}\) is the total flow leaving bus \(b\) minus the total flow entering it. The right-hand side is the demand at the bus plus the net charging of the storage units located there.

Must-run (e.g., nuclear) output is not a decision variable. Nuclear plants usually run at a constant output, so the model treats them as a fixed injection \(MR_{b,t}\) in the bus power balance. The injection is the must-run capacity at the bus, reduced by the fleet-wide nuclear outage loss divided by the number of nuclear units:

\[MR_{b,t} = \max\!\left(0,\ \overline{MR}_b - \frac{L^{nuc}_t}{N^{nuc}} \right) \qquad \forall b \in \mathcal{B},\ t \in \mathcal{T}\]

The per-unit loss can’t be negative. Because must-run output is fixed, the other resources are dispatched around it.

The dual variable \(\lambda_{b,t}\) of this constraint is the cost of serving one more MW of demand at bus \(b\) in hour \(t\). This is the locational marginal price (LMP), written to duals_file. LMPs differ between buses when transmission limits are binding (i.e., congestion). At buses where load is shed, the LMP is set by the unserved energy penalty (\(\gamma^{LS}\)).

DC power flow#

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} X_l \, f_{l,t} &= S^{base} \sum_{b \in \mathcal{B}} A_{l,b} \, \theta_{b,t} && \forall l \in \mathcal{L},\ t \in \mathcal{T} \\ -\overline{F}_l \le f_{l,t} &\le \overline{F}_l && \forall l \in \mathcal{L},\ t \in \mathcal{T} \\ \theta_{b^{ref},t} &= 0 && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \\ -\pi \le \theta_{b,t} &\le \pi && \forall b \in \mathcal{B},\ t \in \mathcal{T} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

The first equation is the DC power-flow approximation. Since each line has one from bus (\(A_{l,b} = +1\)) and one to bus (\(A_{l,b} = -1\)), the sum equals the angle difference across the line, and the equation can be written as

\[f_{l,t} = S^{base} \, \frac{\theta_{from(l),t} - \theta_{to(l),t}}{X_l}\]

Power flows from the bus with the larger voltage angle to the bus with the smaller one, and lines with lower reactance carry more of the flow. Reactance is given in per unit, so multiplying by \(S^{base}\) = 100 MVA converts the flow to MW. Unlike a transport model, the flow on each line is fixed by the physics of the network once the bus injections are known so the solver can’t route power freely.

The second constraint keeps each flow within the line’s thermal limit in both directions. The third fixes the angle of the reference bus at zero. Only angle differences matter, so without a reference the angles would not be unique. The last constraint bounds the angles to \(\pm\pi\), implemented as \(\pm 3.1415\).

Absolute-flow linearization#

\[\hat{f}_{l,t} \ge f_{l,t}, \qquad \hat{f}_{l,t} \ge -f_{l,t} \qquad \forall l \in \mathcal{L},\ t \in \mathcal{T}\]

The absolute value \(|f_{l,t}|\) is not a linear function, so it can’t appear in an LP directly. These two constraints make \(\hat{f}_{l,t}\) at least as large as both \(f_{l,t}\) and \(-f_{l,t}\). Because \(\hat{f}_{l,t}\) has a positive cost in the objective, the solver pushes it down to the larger of the two, which is exactly \(|f_{l,t}|\).

Generator capacity#

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} p_{g,t} &\le \overline{P}^{O}_{g,t} && \forall g \in \mathcal{G}^{O},\ t \in \mathcal{T} \\ p_{g,t} &\le \overline{P}_g && \forall g \in \mathcal{G}^{D} \setminus \mathcal{G}^{O},\ t \in \mathcal{T} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Coal and gas units are limited by their outage-adjusted capacity \(\overline{P}^{O}_{g,t}\), which changes every hour. All other dispatchable units (oil, biomass, geothermal and hydro) are limited by their nameplate capacity.

The outage-adjusted capacity represents generator outages. The lost_capacity_file gives the total capacity lost in each outage group and hour. The loss is split equally among the generators of the group and subtracted from their nameplate capacity. Capacity can’t be negative:

\[\overline{P}^{O}_{g,t} = \max\!\left(0,\ \overline{P}_g - \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K} , g \in \mathcal{G}_k} \frac{L_{k,t}}{|\mathcal{G}_k|} \right) \qquad \forall g \in \mathcal{G}^{O},\ t \in \mathcal{T}\]

\(\overline{P}^{O}_{g,t}\) is computed before the solve, so the constraint stays linear. Splitting the loss equally is an approximation. The input data says how much capacity a group loses, not which units are out.

Ramp rates#

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} p_{g,t} - p_{g,t-1} &\le R_g && \forall g \in \mathcal{G}^{TH},\ t \in \mathcal{T} \\ p_{g,t-1} - p_{g,t} &\le R_g + \Delta_{g,t} && \forall g \in \mathcal{G}^{TH},\ t \in \mathcal{T} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Thermal units can’t change their output faster than their ramp rate \(R_g\). The first constraint limits how much output can increase from one hour to the next (ramp up), and the second how much it can decrease (ramp down). For the first hour of a horizon, the previous output is the output in the last hour of the previous horizon: \(p_{g,0} = p^{0}_g\). This links consecutive horizons, so a unit can’t jump to a different output level at midnight.

A generator outage can remove capacity faster than the unit could ramp down. Without an adjustment, the capacity limit and the ramp-down limit could then contradict each other and make the problem infeasible. The ramp-down allowance \(\Delta_{g,t}\) relaxes the ramp-down limit by the size of the drop in outage-adjusted capacity.

Both ramp constraints are switched off for \(t = 1\) of the very first horizon of a fresh run, because there is no previous dispatch yet.

Hydropower#

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \underline{H}_g \le p_{g,t} &\le \overline{H}_g && \forall g \in \mathcal{G}^{HY},\ t \in \mathcal{T} \\ \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} p_{g,t} &\le E^{H}_g && \forall g \in \mathcal{G}^{HY} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Hydropower is limited by the water availability, which the model takes as given from the daily hydro input files. These constraints describe what a hydro plant can do with that water:

  • The first constraint keeps the hourly output of every hydro unit between a minimum and a maximum. The minimum \(\underline{H}_g\) represents required releases, such as minimum flows for the environment, that produce electricity whether or not it is needed. The maximum \(\overline{H}_g\) reflects the turbine capacity and water conditions of the day.

  • The second constraint is an energy budget: the total hydro generation over the horizon can’t exceed the energy \(E^{H}_g\) that the available water can produce. Within this budget, the model is free to shift hydro generation to the hours where it saves the most cost, typically the hours of highest demand. Generation may be lower than the budget.

The hourly limits use the values for the day of the horizon’s first hour. The budget is the sum of the daily budgets of all days in the horizon. Hydro units are also subject to their nameplate capacity (see Generator capacity).

Variable renewables#

\[p_{g,t} \le AV_{g,t} \qquad \forall g \in \mathcal{G}^{VRE},\ t \in \mathcal{T}\]

Solar, onshore wind and offshore wind output depends on the weather, so it can’t be scheduled at will. \(AV_{g,t}\) is the generation that the weather allows in each hour, read from the nodal solar, wind and offshore wind files at the bus of each unit.

The constraint is an inequality: a unit can produce up to the available amount but may produce less. The difference, \(AV_{g,t} - p_{g,t}\), is curtailment. Renewable energy is curtailed when using all of it would violate another constraint, for example when transmission lines out of a region are congested or when must-run and minimum hydro output already cover demand.

Storage#

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} e_{s,t} &= e_{s,t-1} + \eta^{ch}_s \, c_{s,t} - \frac{d_{s,t}}{\eta^{dis}_s} && \text{(1) State-of-charge balance} \\ c_{s,t} &\le \frac{\overline{SoC}_s - e_{s,t-1}}{\eta^{ch}_s} && \text{(2) Charging headroom} \\ d_{s,t} &\le \left( e_{s,t-1} - \underline{SoC}_s \right) \eta^{dis}_s && \text{(3) Available energy} \\ \frac{c_{s,t}}{\overline{P}^{ch}_s} + \frac{d_{s,t}}{\overline{P}^{dis}_s} &\le 1 && \text{(4) Joint charge/discharge limit} \\ 0 \le c_{s,t} \le \overline{P}^{ch}_s, \quad 0 \le d_{s,t} &\le \overline{P}^{dis}_s, \quad \underline{SoC}_s \le e_{s,t} \le \overline{SoC}_s && \text{(5) Bounds} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

These hold for all \(s \in \mathcal{S}\) and \(t \in \mathcal{T}\), with \(e_{s,0} = SoC^{0}_s\), the state of charge at the end of the previous horizon. A fresh run starts every unit at its minimum state of charge \(\underline{SoC}_s\).

Storage units (batteries and pumped storage hydro) move energy in time. They charge when power is cheap and discharge when it is expensive. The constraints work as follows:

  1. State-of-charge balance: The energy stored at the end of an hour is the energy at the end of the previous hour, plus the energy added by charging, minus the energy removed by discharging. The round-trip efficiency is \(\eta^{ch}_s \eta^{dis}_s\).

  2. Charging headroom: A unit can’t charge more than fits between the state of charge at the start of the hour and the maximum state of charge, after charging losses.

  3. Available energy: A unit can’t deliver more than the energy stored above its minimum state of charge at the start of the hour, after discharging losses.

  4. Joint charge/discharge limit: Charging and discharging in the same hour share the unit’s power capability. Charging at full power leaves no room for discharging, and vice versa. Together with the storage cycling cost in the objective function, this keeps the model from charging and discharging at the same time.

  5. Bounds: Charging and discharging power are limited by the unit’s power ratings, and the state of charge stays within its limits.

The storage power enters the bus power balance at the unit’s bus such that charging is extra demand and discharging is extra supply.
